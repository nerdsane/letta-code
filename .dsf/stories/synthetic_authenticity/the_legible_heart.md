# The Legible Heart

*A story set in the Synthetic Authenticity world, 2035 NYC*

---

The interface hums against my temples, familiar as breath. I'm three hours into a session, and the morning light through the loft windows has shifted from pale grey to something warmer. On the monitors ringing my workspace, the authentication logs scroll in real-time: emotional valence readings, AI generation timestamps, my selection decisions marked with green checkmarks or red X's. Every choice I make, visible. Legible.

I used to find the logging intrusive. Now it's like the scratch of a pen on paper, the click of a shutter. Part of the process. Part of what makes the work mine.

The piece I'm building is a commission for the Huang Collection—an immersive environment meant to evoke the experience of learning to trust after betrayal. Heavy stuff. The kind of thing I would have painted ten years ago, all dark oils and obvious symbolism. Now I close my eyes, let myself feel the texture of that particular wound, and the system reads what it can: elevated cortisol markers, decreased activity in the neural pathways associated with social approach, something the interface logs as "guardedness, moderate intensity."

The AI takes that and gives me a space: a room with walls that seem solid but aren't quite. When I move through it in preview, my hand passes through what looks like stone. The floor is stable, then suddenly isn't. It's literal, too literal, but it's a starting point.

I reject the first three iterations. The log marks each rejection, timestamps my emotional state at the moment of decision. I was frustrated during the first one, curious during the second, something closer to recognition during the third—close but not right.

The fourth iteration gives me a garden with paths that keep changing. Not dramatically, not obviously. You walk the same route twice and the trees have shifted, barely perceptible. The system has learned something from my frustration, from my "close but not right." It's learned that the violation of trust I'm trying to evoke isn't dramatic. It's the small shifts, the accumulation of tiny uncertainties.

I mark that one for development. The log records my emotional state: satisfaction, curiosity, something the system categorizes as "creative momentum." Whatever that means.

---

My assistant Kenji brings lunch around noon—pho from the place on Church Street, the one that's been here since before the neighborhood became what it is now. He's twenty-four, trained in systems architecture, came to me through a referral from the School of Visual Arts where I sometimes guest lecture. He doesn't make art himself. He makes art possible.

"Dr. Reinholt pinged," he says, setting the containers on the counter away from the equipment. "Wants to know if you can do Thursday instead of Friday for the walkthrough."

Marta Reinholt. Critic, theorist, one of the few people whose opinion I actually care about. She wrote a piece three years ago called "The Paradox of Provenance" that changed how I think about what I do. Her argument: that documentation doesn't capture authenticity, it creates a new kind of authenticity. The logs don't prove the work is "real"—they construct a narrative of realness that viewers can choose to believe.

I found it both validating and destabilizing. Still do.

"Thursday works," I say. "Tell her morning is better. I'm sharper before I start working."

Kenji nods, already composing the message. He's good at this—the logistics of being an artist in a world where the art is only half the job. The other half is managing the documentation, the collectors, the institutions, the endless performance of transparency.

"Also," he says, not looking up from his tablet, "Marcus Chen's assistant called. He wants to preview the Huang piece before it's finished."

I stop mid-bite. "He wants to see an incomplete work?"

"He wants to see the logs from the development phase. Says he's interested in the decision architecture."

Marcus Chen. Hedge fund money, came to art collecting late, approaches it like due diligence on an investment. He bought a piece of mine two years ago—an immersive environment I called *Shoreline*, about grief and memory and the way the ocean keeps coming back no matter what you build against it. At the opening, he spent forty minutes with the provenance viewer, watching my emotional state fluctuate as I built it, and maybe ten minutes actually experiencing the piece itself.

I should find that offensive. Some part of me does.

But another part thinks: at least he's looking. At least he's curious about the process. How many collectors in the old days bought art as status symbols, as investments, without ever really seeing it at all?

"Tell him he can see the final logs when the piece is done," I say. "Not before."

---

Thursday morning, Marta arrives in a vintage coat that probably costs more than my first year's rent in this city. She's sixty-something, silver-haired, sharp in a way that's earned rather than performed. She walks through the studio slowly, looking at the equipment, the screens, the authentication setup, before she looks at me.

"You've upgraded," she says.

"Had to. The new CAP standards require finer granularity. Emotional state readings every 200 milliseconds instead of every second."

"Does it change anything?"

"The logs are denser. Collectors like it. Whether it captures anything more meaningful..." I shrug.

She makes a sound that might be amusement. "The eternal question."

I walk her through the Huang piece, which is about seventy percent complete. The garden with the shifting paths has evolved—now there's a figure in it, always at the edge of vision, never quite visible when you look directly. The AI suggested it based on my emotional inputs; I refined it, made the figure's presence more subtle, more deniable. The kind of thing you might convince yourself you imagined.

Marta experiences it in full immersion, the lightweight glasses showing her the environment as if she's standing in it. I watch her move through the space, her body responding to paths that aren't physically there. After ten minutes, she removes the glasses.

"It's good," she says. "The uncertainty is effective. You feel it in your body before you understand it intellectually."

"That's the goal."

"The AI contribution—what percentage would you estimate?"

I've been asked this question a hundred times, and I still don't have a good answer. "It generates the raw material. I shape it. Is a sculptor responsible for less of a statue because they didn't quarry the marble?"

"Some would say yes."

"Some would. And they're not wrong to ask the question. I just don't think the question has a clean answer."

She nods, thoughtful. "You know, I visited a show in Ridgewood last week. Undocumented work. All of it."

The Ridgewood underground. Artists who reject CAP entirely, who refuse to log their process, who sell through informal networks and private transactions. Some of them are traditionalists who think documentation kills the magic. Others are provocateurs making a point about surveillance and ownership. A few are probably running scams, generating AI work and claiming it's purely human.

"What did you think?" I ask.

"The work was uneven. Some of it was extraordinary. Some of it was obvious fraud. And I couldn't tell which was which." She pauses. "I found that interesting."

"Interesting how?"

"When I look at documented work, I know what I'm getting. I can verify the process, follow the decisions, understand how it was made. With undocumented work, I have to trust. Or not trust. I have to make a choice based on nothing but my own judgment."

"And you liked that?"

"I found it clarifying. I had forgotten what it felt like to just... respond to art. Without the provenance layer. Without knowing." She looks at me directly. "Do you ever miss that?"

The honest answer is complicated. I started logging my work because I had to—after the 2028 scandal, after trust in the art world collapsed, documentation became the price of admission to serious venues. At first it felt like surveillance. Then it felt like protection. Then, gradually, it became part of how I think about making work.

But do I miss the mystery? The not-knowing?

"Sometimes," I say. "I miss the possibility of being misunderstood."

---

That night, I meet my friend Dev for drinks at a bar in the West Village that still has physical menus and doesn't authenticate anything. He's a painter—oils, canvas, brushes, the whole analog toolkit. His work isn't logged because there's nothing to log. No AI involvement, no neural interface, just his hands and his eyes and decades of practice.

"Marta visited," I tell him. "She went to a Ridgewood show."

"Bold for someone who built her career on provenance theory."

"She said she liked not knowing."

Dev swirls his whiskey. He's older than me by a decade, came up in a different world. His work sells well, but not to the same collectors who buy mine. Different market, different values. We've been friends since I was a student and he was guest-teaching a figure drawing class I took for "traditional grounding," which my advisor thought I needed.

"You know what I don't understand about your system?" he says. "You document everything except the thing that matters."

"Which is?"

"Why you make the choices you make. Your logs show that you rejected option one and accepted option four. They show your emotional state at each decision point. But they don't show why. They don't show the thought, the meaning, the intention."

"The technology can't read thoughts. Only emotional states."

"Exactly. So what's being authenticated? Not your creativity. Not your vision. Just your... presence. Your emotional involvement. You could be present and emotionally involved while making terrible choices. The logs wouldn't know the difference."

He's not wrong. It's something I think about more than I admit—the gap between what the authentication system captures and what actually makes art meaningful. The logs prove I was there, that I felt something, that I made decisions. They don't prove those decisions were good. They don't prove I have anything worth saying.

"It's better than nothing," I say, and hear the defensiveness in my own voice.

"Is it? Or is it just a more sophisticated kind of nothing?" He finishes his drink, signals for another. "Look, I'm not criticizing. You've found a way to make work that matters to you, in a system that has rules I don't fully understand. That's what artists do. I just wonder if you've ever questioned whether the rules themselves make sense."

---

The Huang piece opens six weeks later, in a gallery space in Chelsea that's been retrofitted for immersive work. The collector, Eleanor Huang, is there, along with her family—the piece was commissioned as a gift for her daughter, who's going through something difficult that Eleanor was too discreet to name directly. I just knew it involved trust, and loss, and the slow work of rebuilding.

I watch the daughter experience the piece. She's maybe nineteen, early twenties, moves through the shifting garden with a kind of concentrated attention that tells me it's landing. When she encounters the figure at the edge of vision—the presence that's never quite there when you look—she stops. Stands still. Something crosses her face that I can't read.

Afterward, she finds me.

"My mother told me how you made it," she says. "That you felt something, and the system read it, and then you shaped what it gave you. That every decision is recorded."

"That's right."

"Can I see the logs?"

I pull them up on my tablet, hand it to her. She scrolls through the emotional state readings, the timestamps, the decision points. I watch her looking at the record of my process—the frustration in the early iterations, the recognition when the piece started to work, the satisfaction when it came together.

"You were really feeling it," she says. "When you made this."

"I was trying to understand something. The piece is what I learned."

She looks at the tablet for a long moment, then back at the gallery space where the piece is still running, empty now, the garden shifting for no one.

"I think I like knowing," she says. "That someone felt something real while making this. Even if I can't fully understand what they felt. Even if the logs don't capture everything." She hands the tablet back. "It's like... evidence. That there's a person in there somewhere. Behind all the technology."

---

Later, walking home through streets that have changed so much since I arrived in this city fifteen years ago, I think about what she said. Evidence that there's a person in there somewhere.

Is that what authenticity has become? Not proof of sole human creation—that ship sailed years ago, if it was ever really docked. Not proof of genius or vision or meaning. Just proof of presence. Evidence that somewhere in the process, a human being was feeling something.

Maybe that's enough. Maybe that's what it always was, underneath the myths we told ourselves about authorship and originality. Just proof that we were here. That we felt something. That we tried to make it legible for someone else.

The night is cold, and the city is bright around me, and I'm thinking about the next piece already—something about legibility itself, maybe. About the gap between what we can capture and what matters. About the desire to be seen, and the fear of being known.

The logs will record whatever I feel while making it. They'll document my decisions, my emotional states, my process. They won't explain why I care about any of this, or what I'm trying to say, or whether the work is good.

That part is still mine to figure out. And maybe always was.
